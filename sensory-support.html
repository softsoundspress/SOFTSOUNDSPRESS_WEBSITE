SENSORY SUPPORT

Understanding Sensory Differences

Children may experience sounds, lights, textures, movement, smells, or busy environments more intensely than others. Sensory support begins with understanding, observation, and helping children feel safe.

Notice the Triggers

Pay attention to the environments, sounds, textures, movements, or situations that seem to cause discomfort or overwhelm.

Patterns can help you understand what the child may be experiencing and what support may be helpful.

Reduce Overload When Possible

When a child is overwhelmed, reducing stimulation can help.

This might include lowering noise, dimming lights, moving to a quieter space, or reducing visual clutter.

Prepare for Loud Environments

Some children may become overwhelmed by places such as stores, cafeterias, assemblies, public events, or crowded rooms.

Preparing the child beforehand and bringing sensory supports can make these situations easier to navigate.

Create a Calm Space

A calm space can give a child somewhere to regulate when the environment becomes overwhelming.

It might include comfortable seating, reduced noise, familiar objects, soft lighting, or calming activities.

Offer Sensory Tools Thoughtfully

Headphones, fidgets, movement breaks, weighted items, or other sensory tools may help some children regulate.

The goal is to use supports intentionally based on the child's needs rather than assuming one strategy works for everyone.

Watch for Early Signs of Overwhelm

Children may show signs before reaching a point of distress.

These may include covering ears, becoming quieter, increased movement, humming, avoiding an area, becoming irritable, or seeking escape.

Support Regulation Before Correction

When a child is overloaded, they may not be able to process directions or expectations effectively.

Help the child regulate first. Teaching and problem-solving are often more effective after the child feels safe again.

Every Child Is Different

One child may seek movement while another avoids it. One may love certain sounds while another finds them painful.

Sensory support should be individualized around the child's responses, preferences, and needs.

BE CURIOUS, NOT JUDGMENTAL

Behavior Can Be Communication

Sometimes what looks like refusal, frustration, avoidance, or a difficult behavior may actually be a child's response to sensory overwhelm.

Asking "What might this child be experiencing?" can help us respond with greater understanding.
